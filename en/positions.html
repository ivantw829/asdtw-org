POSITIONS

Positions

This page expands on the four core principles on our home page and outlines our positions on specific issues.

This English translation is provided for reference. In the event of any discrepancy, the Traditional Chinese original is authoritative.

1. Centered on Disabled People and Gender/Sexual Minorities

Lived Experience as Knowledge

Lived experience is a legitimate source of knowledge in its own right. We reject the assumption that the experiences of disabled people and gender/sexual minorities must be translated by medical, special education, or academic professionals before being taken seriously. Standpoint theory shows that people situated at the margins are often better positioned to see social structures overlooked by dominant perspectives; our experiences are not only personal stories, but also a way of understanding society.

Self-Identification First

Within our community, we respect self-identification and do not require a formal diagnosis as a prerequisite for participating in discussions or claiming an identity. In Taiwan, obtaining an adult diagnosis of autism or another neurodevelopmental condition often requires significant time, money, and access to medical resources—conditions shaped by class, age, and urban–rural divides. Treating diagnosis as a gatekeeper lets existing inequalities decide who has the right to speak.

2. Nothing About Us Without Us

Parent Organizations Do Not Represent Us

The participation of parents, caregivers, and professionals has its own value, but their views cannot stand in for disabled people themselves. In Taiwan, public debate and policy consultation on autism are frequently dominated by parent organizations and service providers, while autistic people are rarely in the room. We maintain that any policy or service aimed at autistic people must prioritize hearing from autistic people directly.

3. Against Ableism

Against Functioning Labels

Labels such as “high-functioning” and “low-functioning” rank people by how closely they approximate the norm. On one hand, they deny that those labeled “high-functioning” have real support needs; on the other, they deny the agency and capabilities of those labeled “low-functioning,” reducing complex human beings to a single scale. We advocate providing support based on each person’s actual needs in specific contexts rather than classifying people by functioning level.

Against “Overcoming Disability” Narratives

Mainstream media often portrays disabled people as inspirational figures who “overcome” their disability. This narrative frames disability as a personal misfortune to be conquered, shifts society’s responsibility onto the individual, and makes those who do not “overcome” appear insufficiently diligent. Disabled people do not need inspirational stories to prove we deserve respect.

Against Normalization-Based Behavioral Interventions

Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA) is widely promoted in early intervention and special education in Taiwan and has long been called the “gold standard” of autism intervention. Our objection is specific: we oppose interventions aimed at normalization—using rewards, punishments, and repetitive compliance drills to make autistic people appear indistinguishable from non-autistic people. Early ABA pioneer Ivar Lovaas explicitly set being “indistinguishable from their peers” as the goal of therapy, and early behavioral interventions also relied on aversive stimuli such as hitting, shouting, and electric shocks.

We oppose normalization-based interventions for five reasons.

First, the goal is misplaced. Normalization treats “looking non-autistic” as success—eliminating stimming such as hand-flapping or rocking, forcing eye contact, and training children to sit still and obey commands. Most of these behaviors are harmless and serve as ways for autistic people to regulate sensory input and emotions; suppressing them serves the comfort of onlookers, not the well-being of the autistic person.

Second, compliance training comes at a cost. When intervention centers on obeying instructions, children often learn to suppress their own needs and give up the right to refuse. Self-advocates warn that this weakens autistic people’s ability to say “no” and set boundaries—precisely the skills a person needs most to stay safe from harm.

Third, lived experience must be heard. Many autistic adults who underwent ABA in childhood report that it taught them to mask who they are, leaving lasting shame and psychological harm. The Autistic Self Advocacy Network (ASAN) has likewise noted that normalization-oriented approaches are associated with poorer mental health outcomes among autistic adults.

Fourth, the evidence is not as solid as claimed. A major 2023 meta-analysis published in The BMJ found that studies of early autism interventions are largely low in quality and rarely monitor potential adverse effects. A 2024 meta-analysis in JAMA Pediatrics further found that greater intervention intensity did not correspond to better outcomes, undermining the claim that “more hours are better.” This does not mean every intervention is ineffective, but it shows that presenting ABA as the single proven answer is untenable.

Fifth, changing the name does not change the goal. Newer approaches such as Naturalistic Developmental Behavioral Interventions (NDBI) and “neurodiversity-affirming ABA” have emerged in recent years. While we welcome genuine change, we evaluate any intervention by three criteria: who sets the goals, whether the focus is on changing the person or the environment, and whether the person has the right to refuse. Renaming a practice does not alter these standards.

What We Advocate: The Neurodiversity Paradigm

Beyond opposing normalization, we advocate replacing the pathology paradigm with the neurodiversity paradigm. Formulated by autistic scholar Nick Walker, the pathology paradigm assumes there is only one “correct” way for human neurocognition to function and treats divergence as deficit or disease. The neurodiversity paradigm instead views neurodivergence—such as autism, ADHD, and dyslexia—as natural variations in human neurocognition, much like ethnic or gender diversity: neither right nor wrong in itself, with harm arising from social exclusion of difference.

The double empathy problem, proposed by autistic researcher Damian Milton in 2012, challenges the deficit model from another angle. Whereas dominant theories attributed social difficulties to an autistic inability to understand others, Milton pointed out that misunderstandings between autistic and non-autistic people are mutual: non-autistic people also struggle to understand autistic people. Because the difficulty is two-way, accommodation must be two-way as well—autistic people cannot be expected to shoulder all the adaptation alone.

Grounded in these two frameworks, the forms of support we advocate include:

  • Centering the person’s own goals. Support should aim for quality of life, autonomy, and well-being, decided with the person’s participation. For young or nonspeaking children, their preferences and responses must be read carefully to understand their wishes.
  • Respecting all forms of communication. Speech is not the only language; sign language, text, picture cards, and Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC) are all valid forms of communication and should be provided early, without waiting for speech to be “mastered.”
  • Not targeting harmless autistic traits. Stimming, deep interests, and avoiding eye contact do not need to be eliminated when they cause no harm.
  • Understanding what behavior communicates first. Behaviors that genuinely threaten safety, such as self-injury or aggression, must be addressed by first identifying underlying causes—such as pain, sensory overload, communication barriers, or environmental stress—rather than simply suppressing the behavior.
  • Adapting the environment first. Reduce sensory overload, provide predictable routines and quiet spaces, and treat reasonable accommodation as the first step of support.
  • Teaching self-advocacy. Support autistic people in expressing their needs, refusing what they do not want, and knowing their rights.
  • Building mutual understanding. Training for teachers, parents, and professionals should teach them how to understand autistic communication, not only how to train autistic people to conform.
  • Co-designing with autistic people. Support services, research, and professional training should involve autistic people from the outset—putting “Nothing About Us Without Us” into concrete practice.

Intersectionality

Systems of oppression based on disability, gender, sexual orientation, class, ethnicity, and age do not operate in isolation; they intersect. The realities faced by an autistic transgender person cannot be reduced to the challenges of being autistic plus those of being transgender—the two identities compound in healthcare, education, family life, and the workplace, producing forms of exclusion invisible to a single-axis lens. Intersectionality is the theoretical foundation for centering both disabled people and gender/sexual minorities. In crip theory, where queer theory and disability studies meet, compulsory able-bodiedness and compulsory heterosexuality are understood as mutually reinforcing norms. Nick Walker, who is both autistic and queer, further introduced the concept of “neuroqueer,” arguing that neurocognitive norms, like gender norms, can be questioned and loosened. Accordingly, whenever we examine an issue, we ask how it affects people holding multiple marginalized identities.

Accessibility Is a Right

Accessibility and reasonable accommodation are rights that belong to disabled people—not favors or special charity granted by society. Through its Enforcement Act, the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) carries domestic legal force in Taiwan, obligating governments, schools, workplaces, and public services to remove barriers and provide accommodations. Treating accessibility as optional goodwill means it can be withdrawn at any time; we reject that framing.

4. Committed to Praxis

Inclusive Education

Disabled students have the right to learn alongside their peers in general education settings. Schools should adapt curricula, assessments, environments, and systems rather than demanding that students change themselves to fit the school. Inclusion also does not end with placing a student in a general classroom; schools must provide adequate support staff and reasonable accommodations.

Public Participation

We take part in policy discussions, public hearings, social movements, and public discourse to bring directly affected perspectives into public decision-making. Articles written by members, actions on the ground, and responses to policy are how we turn “Nothing About Us Without Us” into reality.
