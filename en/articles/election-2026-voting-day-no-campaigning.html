No Campaigning All Election Day: A Guide to Article 56(2) of the Election and Recall Act

From midnight on November 28, do not post anything on any platform that campaigns for a candidate, a party or a recall.

November 27, 2026 · By Formosa Autistic Commune · ~6 min read

This is a translation of an article originally written in Traditional Chinese. If the two differ, the 中文原文 (Chinese original) prevails.

From midnight on November 28, do not post anything that campaigns for a candidate, a party or a recall. Facebook, Instagram, Threads, Bluesky, LINE groups and Discord all count.

Tomorrow is election day for Taiwan's nine-in-one local elections. Article 56, Subparagraph 2 of the Civil Servants Election and Recall Act prohibits political parties and any person from engaging in campaigning, canvassing or recall activities on election day. Since the 2023 amendment, an ordinary person who violates it can be fined NT$100,000 to NT$1,000,000, and candidates, parties and their staff face higher fines. Our previous article, Legal Reminders Before and After the Nine-in-One Vote, touched on this rule briefly. This article covers the text of the law, its scope and actual penalty cases in more detail.

This article is not legal advice

This article only summarizes the statutes, interpretations by the Central Election Commission (CEC) and its administrative appeal decisions. For specific situations, rely on the original text of the law and the CEC's interpretations. If in doubt, ask the CEC or your local election commission directly.

The law and the penalties

Article 56 lists four things that political parties and any person may not do. Subparagraph 2 is "engaging in campaigning, canvassing or recall activities on election day." According to a CEC interpretation, "election day" begins at midnight on the day of the vote. So it does not start when polling stations open, and it does not end once you have voted. The article also says "any person," with no distinction between candidates and ordinary voters, so personal accounts are covered too.

The penalty is in Article 110, Paragraph 6. Before the amendment of June 9, 2023, every violation of this rule was fined NT$500,000 to NT$5,000,000. Since the amendment there are two tiers. Political parties, candidates, the lead proposer of a recall, the person being recalled, and their employees, agents or other persons acting for them are fined NT$200,000 to NT$2,000,000. Everyone else is fined NT$100,000 to NT$1,000,000. Anyone who does not stop when told to is fined again for each further violation. Many explainers online still say "NT$500,000 to NT$5,000,000." Those are the old pre-amendment figures and no longer apply.

What counts as campaigning or canvassing

The CEC's definition is broad. Anything done to get yourself elected or to help a particular candidate get elected counts, whatever the method. For a recall, it is anything that seeks support for or opposition to the recall. The purpose of the law is to let voters calm down on election day and decide for themselves, so promotion aimed at specific people is just as unlawful as promotion aimed at the general public.

Examples given by election commissions include canvassing in or near a polling station, handing out flyers, putting slogans or flags back up, using campaign vehicles or loudspeakers, and lingering near a polling station in a candidate's campaign vest. The same goes online: promotion by text message, LINE, Facebook and similar channels is prohibited on election day. Even if the post was not made by the candidate, it is still a violation if it contains a candidate's name, a party's name, a ballot number or a slogan, so that an ordinary person can tell at a glance whom it is promoting.

We think a simple reminder to vote that mentions no candidate, party or recall is less likely to fall within this definition. But neither the statute nor the interpretations draw a clear line, and the cases in the next section show that posts like "I've voted" have been found in violation. To be safe, hold off on any election-related posts, shares and stories on election day, and say what you want to say on the 29th.

Past penalty cases

All of the following are from CEC administrative appeal decisions. The fines were imposed under the old, pre-amendment penalties, so the amounts are higher than they would be now.

  • On the day of the 2021 vote to recall legislator Chen Po-wei, a Taichung city councilor posted "Fight the malicious recall, go monitor the count!" on their Facebook page, urging people to go to polling stations to monitor the counting. The election commission found that this clearly expressed opposition to the recall and imposed a fine of NT$500,000. The appeal was dismissed.
  • On the day of a recall vote, a member of the public posted that they had voted and that the weather was nice, arguing that they had not meant to encourage anyone to vote. The CEC found that the post was asking those who saw it to go and vote quickly. The person later changed the post to "Cast my vote this afternoon! Please come out and vote!" The NT$600,000 fine was upheld.
  • On the day of the 2020 vote to recall Han Kuo-yu, a member of the public published a 27-character public post. They argued that they were not from Kaohsiung, had no stake in the outcome, and did not know about this law. The CEC found that because the post was public and visible to an unspecified number of people, it was a violation regardless of length, and upheld the statutory minimum fine of NT$500,000.
  • A member of the public who shared a candidate's post on election day, saying it was just a habit of sharing posts as a record, was likewise found in violation. Every one of these cases argued "I didn't know the law," but under the Administrative Penalty Act, not knowing the law does not exempt you from a penalty. It can only reduce the penalty depending on the circumstances.

Should I delete posts from earlier in the week?

A CEC interpretation holds that if you do no new promotion on election day and lawful content you posted earlier simply remains online into election day, Article 56, Subparagraph 2 does not apply in principle. So you do not necessarily have to delete posts from the past few days. However, if that state of affairs can be seen as a new act in its own right and reflects the person's intent, such as re-pinning, editing or re-sharing an old post on election day, it may still be found to be a violation. In the end it depends on the facts of each case.

Discord and LINE groups are not beyond the law. Campaign messages in groups count too. Community moderators may want to remind everyone on the 28th not to discuss specific candidates for now, and to talk it over properly once the results are in.
