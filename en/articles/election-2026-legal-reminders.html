Legal Reminders Before and After the Nine-in-One Vote: Poll Blackout and No Election-Day Campaigning

The two easiest ways for ordinary people to break the law: leave polls alone from November 18, and do not campaign on election day, November 28.

November 17, 2026 · By Formosa Autistic Commune · ~4 min read · Updated November 18, 2026

This is a translation of an article originally written in Traditional Chinese. If the two differ, the 中文原文 (Chinese original) prevails.

Voting in Taiwan's 2026 nine-in-one local elections takes place on Saturday, November 28. Restrictions during the election period are mainly set out in the Civil Servants Election and Recall Act (the "Election and Recall Act"). Fines start at NT$100,000, and posting, sharing and commenting online all count, including chatting in our server. Below are the rules ordinary people most need to know.

This article is not legal advice

This article only summarizes the Election and Recall Act and explanations from the Central Election Commission (CEC) to give you a quick overview. For specific situations, rely on the original text of the law and the CEC's interpretations.

From November 18: leave polls alone

From 00:00 on November 18 until voting ends, you may not publish, report, distribute, comment on or cite election polls by any means. Sharing, posting screenshots, commenting that "so-and-so is ahead in the polls," or commenting on polls published earlier all count. Online communities are no exception: the CEC has warned that discussing poll data in online groups also breaks the law. Violators are fined NT$100,000 to NT$1,000,000 if they are ordinary people, and NT$200,000 to NT$2,000,000 if they are political parties or candidates (Articles 53 and 110).

Only polls are banned, though. Other election topics are not. Claims circulating online that "you can't share any election information after the blackout" are wrong: until election day, you can still discuss policy platforms and comment on candidates.

Election day, November 28: no campaigning

On election day, from 00:00 until voting ends, you may not engage in any campaigning or canvassing (Article 56). The CEC interprets this broadly: anything intended to help a particular candidate get elected counts, regardless of form or length. Violators are fined NT$100,000 to NT$1,000,000 if they are ordinary people, and NT$200,000 to NT$2,000,000 if they are political parties or candidates (Article 110).

These are real cases where people were fined:

  • On election day, someone sent a message to a LINE group reminding everyone to vote and asking them to canvass friends and family for a particular candidate. They were fined NT$250,000.
  • On the morning of election day, someone reshared a campaign event post that another person had published the day before. They were also penalized, because resharing counts as posting it yourself.
  • On the day of the 2025 recall votes, someone posted on Threads urging people to vote "No." The election commission fined them, and their administrative appeal was dismissed.
  • Someone argued that their post was only 27 characters long. The CEC replied that campaigning and canvassing on election day are prohibited in any form.

At the polling station

After marking your ballot, you may not show it to anyone. You also may not bring a switched-on phone or any recording equipment into the polling station. Violators are fined NT$30,000 to NT$300,000, and using recording equipment to find out how a ballot was marked is punishable by up to five years in prison (Articles 65 and 106). Taking a ballot out of the polling station also carries criminal liability.

You may not wear a party's or candidate's insignia inside the polling station, and refusing to leave when asked to carries criminal liability. The CEC has also reminded voters that a face mask worn into the polling station must not bear a candidate's name, ballot number, photo or campaign slogan. Within 30 meters outside the polling station, you may not make noise or disrupt voting, and continuing after being told to stop also carries criminal liability (Article 108).

Throughout the election period

Spreading rumors or false information in order to get a candidate elected or defeated, in a way that is capable of causing harm, is punishable by up to five years in prison, or up to seven years if deepfake audio or video is used (Article 104). Casually forwarding something can also bring legal liability, so please check before you forward. In practice, however, the courts hold that if someone had reasonable grounds to believe something was true and spread it for that reason, the offense is not made out, because there was no criminal intent.

Vote buying also carries criminal liability. Offering or giving a bribe to a person with the right to vote is punishable by three to ten years in prison, and a fine may also be imposed (Article 99). Running an election betting operation is punishable by up to five years in prison plus a fine of up to NT$500,000, and people who place bets can also face up to six months in prison, short-term detention or a fine of up to NT$100,000. In addition, other rules, such as defamation under the Criminal Code and the ban on spreading rumors under the Social Order Maintenance Act, continue to apply during the election period.
